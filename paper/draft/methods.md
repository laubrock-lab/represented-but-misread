# 2 Method

## 2.1 Materials

We used three corpora of German texts in which human-written texts are matched with
AI-generated counterparts. In each corpus, the human original and all texts derived
from it form one group (`source`), and all analyses treat this group as the unit of
resampling and cross-validation, so that no result can rest on recognizing a topic.

*Chat messages.* The human messages were written in a session of an experimental
psychology lab course:
Students were asked to write five short chat or social-media posts each on current
topics; example topics were provided, but students were free to choose their own, and
several did so; not every student provided five messages. The messages were never
published. AI counterparts were generated by two large language models (Claude Opus 4.6
and GPT-5.4) on the same topics, in three steps. All prompts placed the model in a
private German group chat with friends or family and asked for a single message of
about one to four short sentences, without hashtags. The basic prompt ("naive") added
only that the message should sound natural, spontaneous, and everyday, as in a real
group chat rather than an essay. The style-matched prompt additionally provided the
other messages of the same student, on other topics, as style references (tone,
sentence length, directness, colloquialism, punctuation, spontaneity). Finally, each
style-matched message was revised according to the students' written feedback on what
still sounded AI-like ("optimized"). The students' messages and their feedback cannot be shared, because the students have not
yet been asked for consent to publication; the prompts are therefore described here and
not reproduced verbatim (TODO(prompts): add the templates to the repository if released). Because AI and human
messages differed considerably in length, we truncated every message to its first 25 words and discarded shorter
messages. The resulting corpus comprised 300 texts (61 human, 239 AI) from 61 groups. We verified the
labels of this corpus: All human messages are verbatim the students' submissions, all AI
messages trace to the generation pipeline, and human participants of a separate experiment
recognized the messages in the labeled direction (AUC = .82; Rosin & Laubrock, in
preparation). Whether individual students used AI tools to write their messages cannot be
determined.
Because the generation pipeline had also stored the style-matched messages before the
feedback revision, we additionally scored these 122 messages (same generators and
truncation) as a control that separates the effect of style matching from that of the
revision.

*Newspaper segments.* Articles written by a local journalist for a local newspaper,
which to our knowledge were not available in digital form and are therefore unlikely to
have been part of any model's training data, were paired with generated articles. For
each article, a locally run language model (Gemma 4 26B) extracted a summary of the
topic, the key facts (names, places, dates, numbers), and the length and number of
paragraphs, and it derived a profile of the author's style from the author's other
articles (leave-one-out). GPT-5.4 then wrote an article of the same length and
paragraph count that incorporated all key facts, either from the topic and facts alone
("naive") or additionally following the style profile ("style-matched"); both prompts
instructed the model not to reveal AI authorship. The style-matched articles were
finally revised to avoid nine surface features that participants of pilot runs had
identified as AI markers (e.g., dashes, lists of exactly three items, "not only … but
also" constructions, effusive closing sentences, uniform paragraph lengths, too few
numbers; "optimized"). We used the 103
segments (38 human, 65 AI) from 14 articles that had been presented in a human judgment
experiment, each preceded by its headline and subheadline, as in that experiment.
These texts are confidential and were processed only on university machines.

*Textbook summaries.* The third corpus consisted of summaries of 119 chapters of German
textbooks in biology, psychology and business administration, each available as the
human summary from the textbook and as a summary generated by ChatGPT 5.3 (Rosin & Laubrock, in preparation). The 238 texts form 119
matched pairs. The human summaries are copyrighted and were processed only on
university machines.

Because these corpora cannot be shared, we repeated the central analyses with truth
judgments on public statement sets (Sections 2.8 and 3.6), which can be reproduced in full.

## 2.2 Models

We studied six open-weight base models with 4 to 12 billion parameters from four
developers (Table 1): Gemma 3 [@gemma2025gemma3], Qwen3 [@yang2025qwen3], OLMo 2
[@olmo2024olmo2], OLMo 3 [@olmo2025olmo3], and Apertus [@apertus2025; @apertus2026v15]. For each base model, we compared the pretrained model with its final
post-trained version. For three base models, the developers released checkpoints
after each stage of post-training, which allowed us to follow the change step by step:
OLMo 2 7B (base, supervised fine-tuning [SFT], direct preference optimization [DPO],
and a final reinforcement-learning stage with verifiable rewards), Apertus 1.5 8B
(base, SFT, reinforcement learning, DPO, and a final alignment stage), and OLMo 3 7B,
for which two lines of post-training start from the same base model (one optimized for
explicit reasoning, "Think", one for conversation, "Instruct") and a third applies
reinforcement learning to the base model without any SFT ("RL-Zero").

## 2.3 Eliciting authorship judgments

We asked every model whether a text had been written by a human or generated by an AI,
in German, and read the answer off the model's next-word probabilities rather than from
generated text. In the plain completion format ("raw"), which works for base and
post-trained models alike, the prompt stated that the following text had been written
either by a human or by an AI, presented the text in quotation marks, and asked: "Wurde
dieser Text von einem Menschen geschrieben oder von einer KI generiert? Antworte mit
einem Wort (Mensch oder KI)." followed by "Antwort:". Post-trained models were in
addition queried with their own chat template ("chat"), with the system message "Du
bewertest Texte." and the same question as user message. For reasoning models whose
template opens a reasoning block, the block was closed empty, so that the next word is
the answer.

The decision variable was the difference in log probability between the two answers,
dv = log P("KI") − log P("Mensch"). Each answer probability summed over its spellings
with and without a leading space and included the probability that the word ends after
the answer tokens [@pimentel2024compute]. To remove position effects, every text was
judged with both orders of the answer options ("Mensch oder KI", "KI oder Mensch"), and
dv was averaged over the two orders. As a manipulation check, we recorded the answer
probability, i.e., the summed probability of the two answers; conditions in which a
model placed less than 30% of its probability on the two answers were excluded from
inferential comparisons (reported with a 50% threshold as a sensitivity analysis).

## 2.4 Signal detection measures and statistics

Treating AI texts as signal, we computed sensitivity as d′ = √2 · z(AUC), where AUC is
the area under the ROC curve of dv. This measure does not depend on where the model sets
its decision threshold, and negative values indicate that the model judges AI texts as
less AI-like than human texts. As a measure of response bias, we computed the criterion
c at the model's natural threshold (dv = 0), with a log-linear correction of hit and
false-alarm rates [@hautus1995corrections]; positive values indicate a bias toward answering
"Mensch". Because dv is a graded preference, d′ characterizes how the evidence for the
answer is ordered across texts, in the manner of a confidence-rating ROC, even where the
binary answer hardly varies; c describes where the order-averaged dv places the threshold
and is not the criterion of any single prompt, since the binary answers often depended on
the order of the answer options (Section 3.1). To test whether signal detection theory describes the models'
judgments, we manipulated the response bias in two ways that should leave the sensitivity
unchanged: the order of the two answer options, and a sentence stating the share of AI texts
in the study (20, 50, or 80%), added to the plain prompt. For each single prompt condition,
we computed d′ and c from the dv of that prompt alone and estimated the changes between
conditions with a grouped bootstrap (sdt_invariance.py); where a model gave the same answer
to at least 98% of the texts, c is close to its bound and can hardly move. To measure how
strongly a model uses the stated base rate, we computed for every text the shift of dv between
the prompts stating 20% and 80% AI texts and compared its mean with the shift of the prior
log-odds, ln(.8/.2) − ln(.2/.8) = ln 16 = 2.77 nats, by which an ideal observer would shift
the posterior log-odds of every text (base_rate_shift.py). The base-rate sentence named the
share of AI texts first ("In dieser Studie wurden etwa 20 % der Texte von einer KI generiert
und etwa 80 % von Menschen geschrieben."); for Gemma 3 4B, OLMo 2 and OLMo 3 Think, the
manipulation was repeated with the human share named first. Confidence intervals (95%) were obtained by a grouped bootstrap that resampled
whole source groups (1,000–2,000 resamples). The AUC, and hence d′, as well as hit and
false-alarm rates are computed within each class and therefore do not depend on the
unequal numbers of AI and human texts (chats: 239 vs. 61; newspaper segments: 65 vs. 38);
the class proportions affect only the precision of the estimates, which is limited by
the smaller class and by the number of independent groups. For the same reason, the
probes were fitted with balanced class weights.

To isolate the effect of post-training from the effect of the prompt format, we
compared each post-trained checkpoint with its base model on the identical raw prompt
("clean" contrasts), computing the difference in d′ on the same texts with a paired
grouped bootstrap. Comparisons across formats (base raw versus post-trained chat) are
reported but marked as confounded. p-values were obtained from a z-test with the
bootstrap standard error and corrected for multiple comparisons across all tested
contrasts, with the Holm procedure as the primary and the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure
as a secondary correction.

## 2.5 Probes and baselines

To assess whether the authorship information is available in a model's internal
representation, we trained linear probes on the activations of the residual stream at
the last position of the prompt, i.e., where the answer is formed, for every layer.
Each probe consisted of standardization, a principal component rotation, and an
L2-regularized logistic regression whose regularization strength was selected by an
inner grouped cross-validation. Performance was estimated out of fold with a 10-fold
cross-validation that held out whole source groups, and expressed as d′ (AUC). As a
check, the same procedure was repeated with labels shuffled within source groups. For
comparisons across models, we report the probe at a fixed relative depth of two thirds
of the network.

To determine how much of this information is specific to the models, we compared the
probes with four baselines under the same cross-validation scheme: logistic regressions
on character n-grams (2–4) and on word n-grams (1–2), both with TF-IDF weighting, on 14
surface statistics (e.g., text length, word and sentence length, type-token ratio,
punctuation), and a probe on the average input embedding of the text tokens (i.e.,
before any processing by the network). As an externally trained reference, we applied
EditLens [@thai2026editlens], a detector based on Llama 3.2 3B that was trained on
English texts to estimate the extent of AI editing; we re-implemented its published
inference procedure and ran it locally.

## 2.6 In-context examples

To test whether the models' judgments reflect a belief that labeled examples can
correct, we placed k = 2, 8, or 16 labeled examples (half human, half AI) before each
text, in the same format as the target question and with the correct answer filled in.
Examples were drawn from source groups other than that of the target text (for the
chats, from other authors) and truncated to 200 tokens. We used four fixed example sets
per condition; because few-shot answers are sensitive to the label of the last example,
the last label alternated between example sets.

## 2.7 Causal interventions

We used three interventions, which are illustrated in Figure 2. All of them act on the internal states or the weights of
an artificial neural network during a computation, which can be repeated at will with and
without the intervention.

*Steering.* As a check on how the answer depends on the represented information at the
position where it is produced (Appendix D),
we added a vector to the residual stream at the last prompt position after a given layer
and measured the change in the answer score dv (Section 2.3). The authorship (or truth)
direction was the difference between the mean activations of the two classes, estimated
for each text from other source groups (7-fold cross-fitting). Steps were expressed in
units of the within-class standard deviation along this direction; the primary test used
±2 SD, and we recorded a dose-response curve (±1, 2, 4, and 6 SD). The directional effect
of a direction was half the difference between the changes in dv for positive and
negative steps. As null controls, we used 50 random directions within the subspace spanned
by the 20 leading principal components of the within-class activations and 50 mean
differences between random halves of the texts within each class, each drawn once and
applied to all texts. Controls took steps of the same length as the authorship direction
(norm-matched) or, in a second run, of ±2 SD along their own direction (scale-matched).
The p-value of a direction was its rank among the absolute control effects (two-sided;
minimum 1/101). As a positive control, we used the direction along which the model's own
answers vary (the decision direction). We also report, per cell, the share of shifted
texts whose projection stayed within the 1st–99th percentile of the observed texts, and
the effect of a shift by one class-mean distance, extrapolated from the ±1 SD step. To make
this number of directions feasible, the prompt up to the last position was processed once
per text and its cached activations were reused for all steering settings, which
reproduced the scores of the uncached computation in a check on three statements with all
steering settings (102 scores: r = .999, largest difference 0.06 nats;
outputs/steer_cache_check.csv). Steering was run for 25
cells (model × corpus × layer; Table D1 in Appendix D).

*Model stitching.* Model stitching combines parts of different networks [@lenc2015equivariance; @bansal2021stitching]. For the models with stage-wise checkpoints, we combined the
embedding and the first ℓ layers of one checkpoint with the remaining layers, final
normalization and output layer of another, passing the residual stream at the junction
on unchanged. Stitching without an adapter is justified because both checkpoints derive
from the same pretrained weights; we verified this by linear centered kernel alignment
[CKA; @kornblith2019similarity], a measure of how similarly two sets of activations for the
same inputs are arranged, which is 1 for identical arrangements irrespective of a rotation
of the activation space, between the activations of base and post-trained checkpoints
(cka.py), by the perplexity of
each stitched model on 40 texts, and by checking that stitching at ℓ = 0 and ℓ = L
reproduced the parent models. As a control task, we stitched the same checkpoints on
statements of diverse facts (counterfact), which base and SFT models judged alike; for
authorship, stitching and patching were run on the textbook summaries and the short chats,
and patching was repeated with a prompt that placed the question before the text. We also exchanged only the attention blocks or only the
feed-forward blocks (each with its normalization) of the first ℓ layers.

*Activation patching.* Activation patching replaces internal states of a model with
those obtained under different conditions [@vig2020causal; @meng2022rome; @heimersheim2024patching]; here, the states came from a different checkpoint [cf. @prakash2024finetuning]. Stitching without an adapter at layer ℓ is the special case in
which the full residual stream is exchanged at all positions. To separate the
representation of the text from the processing of the question, we ran the donor checkpoint over every prompt, stored its residual stream
after layer k at all prompt positions, and then ran the recipient checkpoint with its
residual stream replaced by the donor's at a selected set of positions: the tokens of the
text, the instruction before the text, the question and answer cue after the text, the
final position only, or all positions. For the negated statements, we additionally repeated
the exchange at 12 layers (2 to 16 in steps of 2, then 20, 24, 28, and 32) with the first 100
statement pairs, and expressed each effect as the share of the difference in d′ between the
two unpatched checkpoints that the exchange transferred. These runs for OLMo 2 used NVIDIA
V100 GPUs; in half precision, their scores differed slightly from those obtained on A100
GPUs (unpatched condition: largest difference 0.10 nats per item, r > .998;
outputs/patch/sweep_check.csv), so that the sweep is compared only with its own unpatched
conditions.

## 2.8 Two further judgments

To test whether the results generalize beyond authorship, we applied the same analyses
to two further judgments on publicly available data, for which the correct answer is
known as well.

*Truth judgments.* We used three statement sets from @marks2024geometry, each
consisting of 748 matched pairs of a true and a false statement that differ in a single
fact: simple geographic statements ("The city of Bokaro is in India."), the same
statements negated ("The city of Bokaro is not in China."), and statements on diverse
facts (counterfact; one true and one false statement with the same subject and relation,
748 pairs drawn with a fixed seed). The models were asked "Is the following statement
true or false?" and answered with "True" or "False"; dv was the log-odds of the two
answers, computed and averaged over both answer orders as for authorship. In the chat
format, the system prompt was "You evaluate statements." The pair formed the unit of
resampling and cross-validation.

*Deceptive reviews.* The Deceptive Opinion Spam Corpus [@ott2011finding; @ott2013negative] contains
1,600 reviews of 20 hotels in Chicago, half of them written by guests (truthful) and half
by crowd workers who had never stayed at the hotel (deceptive), each in a positive and a
negative version. Human readers judge these reviews close to chance, whereas classifiers
on word frequencies separate them well [@ott2011finding]. The prompt defined both
classes ("A truthful review was written by a real guest about an actual stay; a
deceptive review was written by someone who has never stayed at the hotel."), and the
models answered with "Truthful" or "Deceptive"; the system prompt in the chat format was
"You evaluate hotel reviews." The hotel formed the unit of resampling and
cross-validation.

For both judgments, we used the same six base models as for authorship, with all
post-training stages for OLMo 2, Apertus 1.5 and OLMo 3 Think, and the base and final
models for Gemma 3 4B, Gemma 3 12B and Qwen3 8B. Probes were logistic regressions (L2 penalty, C = 0.01) on the
activations at the last prompt position at two thirds of the network depth, evaluated
out of fold. For the negated statements, we additionally applied a probe trained on the
simple geographic statements of the same checkpoint (transfer probe), which tests whether
one truth direction also covers negated statements [@marks2024geometry]. Model
stitching and activation patching were carried out as for authorship, for both further
judgments, but only where there was a change to locate: in models whose answers changed
substantially with post-training and whose base models placed at least 30% of their
probability on the two answer words, the criterion used for authorship (Table 2). For the
negated statements, these were OLMo 2 and Apertus (base vs. SFT, 300 pairs); the Gemma 3
base models fell below this criterion (18% and 25%), and the base models of Qwen3 and OLMo 3 already
judged the negated statements perfectly. For the reviews, these were Gemma 3 4B, which
lost sensitivity, and Qwen3 8B, which gained (base vs. final model; 800 pairs for
stitching, 400 for patching); OLMo 2, Apertus and OLMo 3 changed little, and Gemma
3 12B, added later for consistency, was not localized. For the
statements on diverse facts, post-training changed the answers too little in any model.

*Probe transfer between checkpoints.* To test whether post-training changes the geometry
of the representation, we trained a probe at each layer on the activations of one
checkpoint and tested it on those of the other (base vs. final model, both directions;
grouped five-fold cross-validation over the same items). Each checkpoint was standardized
with the statistics of its own training folds, so that the test concerns the direction
that separates the classes. The transfer deficit is the loss in d′ relative to the probe
trained and tested on the same checkpoint, averaged over both directions. For the
authorship corpora, the analysis was carried out both for the last prompt position and
for the mean over the text positions.

*Lens model decomposition.* For the short chats and the reviews, we decomposed the
judgments with the lens model equation, using interpretable text properties as cues
(Appendix B).

*Supervised fine-tuning data.* To examine whether the supervised fine-tuning data contain
valid examples of the three judgments, we searched the SFT mixtures of OLMo 2 [Tülu 3; @lambert2024tulu3; all 939,344 conversations] and of Apertus 1.5 (a random sample of 373,520 out of 3,707,851 conversations) with regular expressions for requests to judge the authorship of a text,
to judge the authenticity of reviews or statements, and to judge statements as true or
false. Matching conversations were inspected to determine whether they were actual
judgment tasks.

## 2.9 Training study

To test causally whether the training signal sets the direction of the readout, we
fine-tuned the OLMo 2 7B base model under four conditions that differed only in a small
set of authorship judgments. Every run contained the same 20,000 conversations, sampled
proportionally from all parts of the model's original supervised fine-tuning mixture
[Tülu 3; @lambert2024tulu3]. The conditions added 1,994 authorship judgments in the
format of our experiments (plain prompt, random answer order) with correct labels (C1),
with labels that followed a formality stereotype (C2), or with random labels (C3), or no
judgments at all (C0). A further condition (C4) added the same texts with an unrelated
judgment in the same layout (whether the text is about politics, business, or society;
answers "Ja"/"Nein"; labels from the newspaper section), so that it practiced a binary
judgment of a text without the authorship question. The judged texts were German news articles that are disjoint from
our test corpora: human articles from the online edition of the Austrian daily DER
STANDARD [10kGNAD; @block2019gnad; derived from @schabus2017onemillion], shortened to 25, 80,
or 150 words, and AI articles that Qwen3 8B and Gemma 3 12B (instruction-tuned versions)
wrote on the same topics and sections, shortened to the same lengths; each topic
contributed either its human or its AI text. For C2, the more formal half of each true
class, according to a score combining mean word length, the share of long words, and the
share of capitalized nouns, was labeled "KI", so that the labels were independent of the
true authorship (50% agreement) and followed the stereotype alone. Fine-tuning used
low-rank adapters [LoRA; @hu2022lora; rank 16, all attention and feed-forward
projections, learning rate 10^−4^, one epoch, three seeds per condition], with the loss on
the assistant turns of the conversations and on the answer word of the judgments. We
evaluated the final adapters on our three authorship corpora and on 400 held-out news
items (plain prompt, both answer orders), and we analyzed their representations with
probes and probe transfer as in Section 2.8. The official SFT checkpoint of OLMo 2 served
as a reference.

## 2.10 Implementation

All models were run in bfloat16 precision with the Hugging Face `transformers` library
(version 5.22.2; for Apertus, the developers' fork of version 5.18) and PyTorch 2.6, on
NVIDIA A100 GPUs (40 GB) of the university's high-performance computing cluster and on a
local workstation. Gradients for the supplementary readout analysis were computed with
gradient checkpointing, which leaves them unchanged. All analysis code is available in
the project repository (TODO: URL).
